India’s Blueprint for AI Regulation

This editorial is based on “The new digital slavery needs constitutional guardrails” published in The Hindu on 29/06/2026. The article emphasises elevating AI governance to a constitutional shield. It highlights that countering algorithmic exploitation, deepfakes, and corporate tech monopolies requires binding legal accountability and structural transparency to preserve human dignity and national sovereignty.

India is proactively confronting the existential threats of unchecked artificial intelligence by implementing a robust, multi-sectoral legislative and policy architecture. Despite progressive measures like the Information Technology Amendment Rules 2026, a structural velocity gap remains between fast-mutating digital harms and traditional legislative mechanisms. To fully protect democratic institutions, governance must transcend minor statutory adjustments and rise to a well-defined constitutional imperative.  

What are the Current Laws and Regulations to Govern AI in India? 
Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Amendment Rules, 2026: Notified by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY), this is the most direct statutory intervention against AI misuse, specifically targeting synthetic content.  
This law defines Synthetically Generated Information (SGI) as computationally created or altered audio, visual, or audio-visual content appearing authentic, explicitly targeting deepfakes and AI impersonation.  
It mandates a 3-Hour Takedown Rule for internet intermediaries and social media platforms to remove unlawful SGI upon notice, which shrinks to 2 hours for non-consensual deepfake or sexually explicit content.  
Furthermore, platforms must enforce Mandatory Labelling & Traceability via technical provenance markers, or face the immediate Loss of Safe Harbour immunity and direct civil and criminal liability.   
IndiaAI Mission & India AI Governance Guidelines: Unveiled by MeitY, this framework provides the overarching structural blueprint for responsible AI adoption across the country, operating on a philosophy of "Innovation over Restraint."  
It mandates that AI systems must adhere to 7 principles, i.e., Trust, People First, Innovation over Restraint, Fairness & Equity, Accountability, Understandable by Design, and Safety, Resilience and Sustainability.  
The guidelines establish specialised national bodies to oversee the ecosystem, including the AI Governance Group (AIGG), the Technology & Policy Expert Committee (TPEC), and an AI Safety Institute (AISI).   
Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023: While not exclusive to AI, this act regulates how AI models are trained in India by restricting the data scraping or usage of personal data to train Large Language Models (LLMs) or neural networks without explicit, unambiguous consent. Non-compliance attracts strict financial penalties.   
Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (BNS), 2023: As India's primary criminal code, the BNS contains provisions that are actively applied to criminal AI offences.  
Provisions regarding digital impersonation, financial fraud, forgery, and defamation are utilised by law enforcement to prosecute individuals who create malicious deepfakes or deploy deceptive automated bots.  
Draft Regulations for Use of Artificial Intelligence in Courts, 2026: Released by the Supreme Court to govern judicial AI deployment, this mandate permits administrative automation (like scheduling or case management) but strictly prohibits AI from exercising core adjudicatory functions like drawing judgments.  
To maintain Judicial Accountability, it explicitly holds judicial officers responsible for any errors or miscarriages of justice caused by "Black Box" algorithms or AI "Hallucinations."    
Framework for Responsible and Ethical Enablement of Artificial Intelligence (FREE-AI): Issued by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), it mandates strict algorithmic audits and risk mitigation protocols for AI systems used in credit scoring, algorithmic trading, fraud detection, and automated customer service.   
What Potential Consequences could Arise from the Unchecked Deployment of AI? 
Proliferation of Deepfake-Enabled Financial Fraud and Voice-Cloning Scams: Generative AI allows malicious actors to execute high-fidelity social engineering attacks. By synthesizing audio and video, fraudsters bypass traditional security protocols like multi-factor or voice authentication.  
According to a report on deepfake financial cybercrime in India, 47% of Indian adults have either been victims of, or know someone targeted by, an AI voice-cloning or deepfake scam—nearly double the global average of 25%.   
Furthermore, 83% of Indian victims suffered direct monetary losses, with almost half losing more than ₹50,000.  
Socio-Cultural Polarization via Low-Cost Proliferation of Hostile Content: Unchecked recommender engines paired with generative content tools create hyper-targeted disinformation pipelines. These systems manipulate public opinion by distributing polarizing narratives at a fraction of traditional advertising costs.  
Empirical evidence shows that AI-driven hostile content can reach targeted audiences for as low as $0.07 per view. This makes the weaponization of synthetic misinformation a highly scalable threat to the fabric of a multi-religious, pluralistic society like India. 
Threat to Human Dignity and Autonomy: AI risks turning the ownership of personal data into a new form of digital subjugation, stripping individuals of their privacy and autonomy. E.g., the Cambridge Analytica scandal showed how Facebook data from millions was harvested to build psychological profiles for targeted manipulation. 
Automated systems are increasingly deciding life-altering outcomes—such as who gets a job, a loan, a medical bed, or an education—without mandatory human oversight or accountability. E.g., Australia's automated Robodebt scheme used a flawed "income averaging" algorithm to wrongly accuse more than half a million welfare recipients of owing debts.  
Corporate Monopoly Power: A small handful of private tech monopolies possess reach and resources that outstrip most sovereign governments, essentially controlling the digital landscape without public check.  
E.g., Google controls 90% of the search market, while Meta reaches billions daily via Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp, and Amazon dominates e-commerce. 
Mutating Regulatory Gap: AI evolves at the breakneck speed of startup culture and mathematical innovation, while democratic lawmaking moves slowly.  
By the time landmark laws such as the European Union’s Artificial Intelligence Act, 2024 or the United Kingdom’s Online Safety Act, 2023 are passed, the technical harms they were built to combat have already mutated, leaving generations vulnerable.   
This rapid momentum is driven not only by corporate competition but also by continuous mathematical breakthroughs. It reveals a profound challenge for global lawmakers: governments can regulate human behavior, but they cannot outlaw the discovery of a mathematical theorem or equation.  
Existential Threats to Democracy: Highly convincing audio and video fabrications of political leaders are strategically deployed during election cycles to depress voter turnout, manufacture scandals, and shatter trust in legitimate state institutions.  
E.g., during India's 2024 general elections, deepfakes of politicians went viral—showing leaders like Indian PM dancing, opponents singing, or even deceased figures endorsing candidates.  
This surge highlights a growing crisis, i.e., over 75% of Indians have encountered deepfakes, yet only 30% feel confident they can spot them.  
Because fear, outrage, and sensationalism drive the most clicks, platform algorithms systematically amplify hyper-partisan content, trapping citizens in customised echo chambers and driving social fragmentation.    
E.g., by prioritising outrage to drive engagement, platform algorithms create partisan echo chambers and rabbit holes (disorienting, or highly engrossing situations) that fuel extreme political polarisation and amplify hyper-partisan voices.  
Mass Structural Job Disruption and Skill Misalignment in Entry-Level Sectors: The rapid automation of cognitive tasks threatens to commoditise entry-level white-collar roles faster than the labour market can transition or upskill, leading to structural unemployment.  
A Cognizant-Pearson's report reveals that 37% of entry-level jobs in India are now being handled by AI, compared to the global average of 33%.  
What Framework is Required to Establish Effective AI Regulation?  
Elevate AI Governance to a Constitutional Imperative: Treating the manipulation of truth as a simple series of software glitches is insufficient. AI governance must be raised to the level of a constitutional shield.  
The right to a clean, unmanipulated information ecosystem where reality can be distinguished from fabrication must be codified as an indispensable extension of the fundamental right to life, liberty, and free expression.  
Implement the Five Pillars of Concurrently Operating Policy: To create an enduring framework—particularly for highly digitalised, pluralistic societies like India—policymakers must execute five structural steps simultaneously:   
A strictly rights-based framework prioritising digital autonomy and personal data protection. 
Structural transparency and accountability for tech platforms, including the removal of absolute safe-harbour immunities for algorithmic amplification. 
Rigid protection of free speech to ensure regulation does not devolve into state-sponsored censorship. 
Nationwide state-backed educational initiatives focusing on media literacy and digital citizenship. 
Advanced, cross-sector early-warning systems to detect and neutralise foreign psychological operations in real-time. 
Risk-Based Classification Tiering: Regulations must establish a dynamic Risk Classification Engine that categorises AI applications by potential harm, avoiding unnecessary friction for low-risk systems while heavily restricting high-risk domains.  
Applications posing an "Unacceptable Risk" (infringe on fundamental rights) such as real-time public biometric identification, untargeted facial recognition data scraping, and algorithmic social scoring, must be prohibited.  
"High Risk" systems in critical sectors like healthcare, infrastructure, credit underwriting, and judicial decision-making require mandatory pre-market certification, while "Minimal Risk" general-purpose tools operate under baseline transparency obligations.    
Mandatory Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) Protocols: To prevent autonomous systemic failures and preserve institutional accountability, the framework must legally enforce Human-in-the-Loop (HITL) architectures within critical workflows.  
Human operators must possess the explicit technical capability and legal protection to override, pause, or terminate autonomous system decisions in real-time if anomalous behaviour is detected.  
Technical Provenance Markers: Generative AI engines must be legally required to inject permanent, tamper-resistant metadata and cryptographic provenance signatures (such as C2PA standards) directly into the binary structure of synthetic media.  
Social media platforms and search engines must deploy automated validation layers capable of reading these provenance markers, enabling mandatory labelling of synthetic content and accelerating the takedown of malicious, non-consensual deepfakes.    
End-to-End Lifecycle Data Hygiene and Auditing: Because an AI model's output is entirely dependent on its input, strict regulatory guardrails must be established at the ingestion phase, matching global frameworks like NIST AI Risk Management Framework (help organisations design, develop, deploy, and use AI systems responsibly).   
Developers must provide documentary proof of data diversity and representative sampling to identify and strip out demographic, racial, or gender-based biases before weights are locked into foundational models.    
Conclusion 
Effective AI regulation requires elevating digital safety into a constitutional shield. India cannot rely on static laws to combat dynamic, fast-mutating algorithms; instead, it must combine rigorous platform liability with technical provenance markers and rights-based protections to preserve human autonomy, social cohesion, and national sovereignty in the digital age.
